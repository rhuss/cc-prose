The open-source movement began in the late 1990s, when proprietary software dominated the industry. Richard Stallman and the Free Software Foundation had been advocating for software freedom for over a decade, but it was Netscape releasing its source code that brought the idea into the mainstream.

The decision was bold. Netscape, once the dominant browser, was losing ground to Microsoft's Internet Explorer. The company's leadership chose to release their codebase to the world, hoping to find a competitive edge they couldn't manufacture internally. It's a moment that gets overlooked in most tech histories, but it changed how the industry thought about collaboration.

What followed surprised even the optimists. Developers started contributing patches, features, and documentation. The Mozilla project grew out of Netscape's remains and eventually produced Firefox, a browser that was rough at first but matured into a real competitor to Internet Explorer. Collective effort, it turned out, could produce polished software.

Open source now runs most of the infrastructure we depend on. Linux powers the majority of servers worldwide. Git has become the default version control system. Both are products of collaborative development, and both trace back, in spirit at least, to that first risky decision to share code with the world.
