The tech industry loves "best practices." It loves them so much it stops asking whether they actually work.

Consider the typical advice given to new engineering managers: communicate clearly, set expectations, provide feedback. Or the startup playbook: move fast, iterate, ship early. These sound wise on slides. They rarely capture the messy reality of building software, because they are not supposed to. They are bumper stickers.

Most engineering decisions are context-dependent. What works at Google does not work at a 12-person startup, and neither translates to a consultancy billing by the hour. But teams keep adopting practices wholesale, without understanding the constraints that made those practices effective somewhere else. That is cargo-cult engineering.

Engineering influencers have made this worse. Simplified frameworks get packaged into blog posts and conference talks, where they sound authoritative and land poorly. A thread that gets 10k likes is not evidence that the advice is good. It is evidence that the advice is tweetable.

I do not have a tidy answer here. Engineering judgment is hard to teach because it depends on things you can only learn by getting them wrong. But I am fairly sure that copying someone else's "three pillars of engineering excellence" is not it.
